I studied abroad in Christchurch, New Zealand from February to May of 2026. It's July 24, 2026 and I'm on the Amtrak from Boston to NYC. I've been meaning to write this up for the month and a half since I've been back in the US, but things have been such a whirlwind that I haven't really had room to breathe until now.

I really didn't like my time abroad, and it's really hard to pinpoint why. I think the short answer is that my priorities and interests didn't match well with the people of New Zealand's South Island. It also didn't help that the South Island is so sparsely populated -- to put it succinctly: New Zealand is a small town country and I am not a small town girl.

Honestly, Christchurch reminded me a lot of high school. There, too, I had felt like a square peg trying my very hardest to fit into various different round holes. There, too, I had just wanted to get away. Here's an excerpt of an email I wrote to Shriya in the middle of the semester: 
"I told you when we last called that I miss the American rat race. I now (with the perspective of 2 whole weeks!) believe it more accurate to say that I miss the little pockets of passionate nerds who I found in America. I'm realizing just how special places like Princeton and SpaceX are, places where I want to hang out with the people around me ALL THE TIME rather than hole up in my room, places where I truly felt that I had found "my people," where I constantly felt inspired and felt the magnitude of what is possible and how much more I can do and how much more I can be. With all the complaining we've done together about 4s Ranch, I don't think it'll be surprising for you to hear me say that I felt out of place back home, but I really did. I forgot if I told you this when we talked, but I was with S*** K*** and I*** over winter break, and she [S*K*] was telling him [I*] about how I used to sit at the lunch table at OVMS and just read my book while everyone was talking about something else around me. I'd entirely forgotten about that. But here in New Zealand, it feels like the middle school lunch table again. I'm ostensibly in the friend group (and I do enjoy their company and think them great people), but like 75% of the time, all I want to do while they're talking is read my book or learn my physics. This is the first time since college that I've felt this way; this disconnect in lifestyle and interests from the people around me who I consider myself closest to. I didn't realize how much of a haven Princeton and Seattle were, how lucky I had been to find such concentrated groups of people who wanted to spend their free time talking about physics or rockets or planes or lasers or history or art or tramp around a forest for no reason. But man, I really, really, miss it. I distinctly remember hanging out with [hometown friends] the week before we all started leaving for college. All of them were lamenting the fact that we had to separate because they really felt that they had found their lifelong friends in high school, and all I could do was sit there and not say anything because I was scared that anything I did say would just reveal how eager I was to get away from everything and everyone back home. (not to say that I don't appreciate them, of course. they are unequivocally my closest friends. but we hold enough differences in interests and lifestyles that i really felt unhappy -- or maybe just unsatisfied? -- with them as my main friend group. even now, i feel so guilty and so unappreciative saying this :(, but I think it's true). And I feel the same way here. My study abroad friends here have mentioned multiple times being so excited to see each other at our schools back in the US after this semester ends, while all I want is to be with my SpaceX friends at Lincoln Labs and come back to my Princeton people again."


Ok, let's break this down into possible reasons why I didn't like being abroad:
1. Lack of Anticipation
	I didn't prepare much to go abroad, nor was I particularly excited for it -- mostly nervous. I didn't think much of this because I had been similarly wary/nervous before going to Japan for vacation just a month prior, and that ended up being some of the most fun 2 weeks of my life. I was hoping New Zealand would work out similarly. I think the lack of anticipation was just the final straw on top of everything else that compounded.

2. Spring Semester
	Spring semester is just always hard. Fall is always the fun one, because everyone's just come back from 2 months of break, the weather is great, and everything is new and shiny again. During spring, I've usually been home in San Diego for a bit (which always puts me in a slightly foul mood -- I dislike the Southern Californian lifestyle immensely), and/or been running around travelling, which means I haven't gotten enough of a break to not be a bit miserable during the work of spring semester.

3. Lack of Full Immersion
	My other extended experience living abroad was my cultural immersion program in Indonesia the summer before I left to college, and my experience on that program could not have been more different than in New Zealand. I came away from that month absolutely buzzing with excitement; that was the best month of my life until that point, I felt that I had finally met "my people", and I sobbed for almost the entire 18 hour flight back because I was so sad to be leaving the group and that country. This whole semester, I was asking myself what was making New Zealand different, and two hypotheses: 1. I wasn't as stressed about deadlines and school and such in Indonesia and 2. I was much more "immersed" in Indonesia. I didn't have my phone for the whole month, and only checked my email a couple times -- program policy to help us better immerse myself. In New Zealand, I was so involved in my life back in the US, even beyond just talking to my friends. I was still planning things for the skating club, figuring out thesis advisor, meeting with my visual arts advisor, and taking summer internship interviews. I was sleeping at 10 every night so I could wake up at 6 and take my meetings with people on the U.S. East Coast, who were a functional 8 hours ahead. This both put me mentally back in the US and prevented me to partaking in any late-night activities (which make up a large portion of college socializing...). I also think that knowing I would be gone in 4 months, and the lack of excitement I had felt for going abroad, preventing me from fully immersing myself and seeking out opportunities to engage with the community as much as I would have if I knew I was staying for longer. 

4. Breakup
	self-explanatory...I really hope this didn't play a very large role, but I suspect that it may have placed some non-negligible undertones...

5. Culture
	I mentioned that I dislike Southern California lifestyle. Unfortunately, I think the lifestyle in New Zealand is a bit like SoCal. New Zealand is known for the dominance of the "life" part of its work-life balance, and this is actually one of the main reasons why people move there. I guess this was my way of stress testing my statement that if all else fails, I'd be happy living a simple life off grid (I would not).

So, I found out that I really like to work. I REALLY like to work, because I want my job to be something that I am very passionate about. Otherwise, why spend 40 hours a week on it??? And also, I want to be good at everything I do, very very good. So I "work" very hard, but I also like working very hard. I like engineering, I LOVE to learn and study/work on projects/take classes. I love all my extracurriculars, love organizing things for the skating club and going to skating practice, love running outdoors events for OA+, love running events for Princeton's service center. There is infinite opportunity to do infinite things at my fingertips. Why would I choose to do anything that I don't enjoy to almost the point of obsession? I meet people like that at Princeton and SpaceX: their work IS their life, not in the sense that it unwillingly takes up all of their time, but because they care about it so much that they want to spend a good portion of their time on it. I'm interested in things that allow me to learn more about the world -- I don't care for buying cosmetic products or standing in line for hours for the newest popular cafe. I enjoy those things once in a while, mostly if I'm doing them with people I like, but I definitely do not like doing them on a regular basis. 

New Zealand is known for its outdoors access, and I love the outdoors, but I learned that love comes second to my love of learning and academic ambitions. Especially towards the second half of the semester, I didn't have the time to take a Friday or Monday off to do a 3-day backpacking trip. Also, almost half of my friend group had little hiking experience, so we couldn't really do any alpine hikes, which are the ones I was most looking forward to. My one good friend whose hiking experience was on par with mines was not a very proactive planner; when I got busy, nothing happened. 

This is how I described my experience to [friend from school]: if my normal emotions take a vaguely sinusoidal form of 7 + sin(wt), where emotions are measured 0 (depression) to 10 (elation), then my emotions in New Zealand were 3 + sin(wt). Similar ups and downs (although there were less frequent ups), but a lower baseline.

I learned how important environment is. I felt so lonely in my ambition: I was often the only one in the animation studio or in my room, working. Again, it felt like high school. I would rather study, even if it meant being solitary. At Princeton and SpaceX and Boston, I don't have to choose between working towards my goals and hanging out with people -- I can often work with other people on either shared or parallel goals. Again, this felt like high school in my loneliness....

I am very, very glad that I went. I learned things about myself and the world that I would have been hard-pressed to learn had I stayed in the Princeton and competitive/high-achieving bubble that I grew up in. Will I be going abroad for an extended period of time soon? Probably not. Would I tell my past self to go abroad? Absolutely, yes.
